# My Life Mission


Every cyberattack is the consequence of a decision. Every decision is shaped by incentives. Every incentive exists within an evolving technological landscape. If these forces can be understood, then the future of cyberspace is not entirely unpredictable.

I did not choose this mission because it is easy, profitable, or popular. I chose it because I believe one of humanity's greatest challenges is that we are forced to defend against a future we do not yet understand.

I refuse to accept that cybersecurity must forever remain reactive. Every day, defenders study what has already happened while attackers imagine what has not. That imbalance is not inevitable. It is a problem waiting to be solved.

My life's work is to build an intelligence that studies the evolution of cyberspace itself. It seeks to understand how innovation, human behavior, economics, geopolitics, and technological progress interact to produce tomorrow's threats. Rather than asking what attackers did yesterday, it asks what they will be compelled to do next.

Its reasoning begins with first principles. It models the evolution of technology, incentives, and complex systems to generate hypotheses about emerging attack pathways. Those hypotheses are evaluated through safe, controlled simulation, refining an ever-deeper understanding of how cyberspace is likely to evolve.

The objective is not merely to detect threats or react to attacks. It is to anticipate the conditions that give rise to them, enabling defenders to prepare before new techniques emerge in the wild.

I know this mission may take decades. It may outgrow the technologies, companies, and even the assumptions that exist today. There will be failures, dead ends, and moments when the destination seems impossibly far away. None of that changes the mission.

This is not just another project.

It is the reason I study cybersecurity.

It is the reason I learn mathematics, artificial intelligence, systems engineering, and computer science.

It is the reason I choose difficult problems over easy victories.

Everything I build, every paper I read, every experiment I run, every line of code I write, and every lesson I learn moves me one step closer to answering a single question:

**Can humanity understand the evolution of cyberspace well enough to anticipate the future instead of merely reacting to it?**

Until that question is answered, my work is not finished.
